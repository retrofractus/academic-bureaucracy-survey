Everyone I know in academia complains about bureaucracy. But most researchers only work at a handful of institutions at best. This makes it hard to know whether the bureaucracy at your institution is better or worse than average.

I created the ABS to provide some quantitative data to back up all the complaining. Now, when you and your peers get together at a conference, you'll know who really is dealing with the most academic BS.

Why this exists

I completed a PhD at Virginia Tech and a postdoc at NYU. Between these two universities, I detected some significant differences in amounts of process required to get things done. But my most revealing experiences occurred when I was working in the grants department at a nonprofit, handling contract negotiations. A few universities were able to turn around countersigned contracts within a couple of weeks. But many took months to get to the point where we could actually send the money.

This information exists in the minds of contract staff, but isn't transparently or publicly available. That creates little incentive for universities to improve. So I decided to try to address this by ranking universities, using actual quantitative information about processes.

Disclosure: I have been affiliated with Virginia Tech and NYU. Both are eligible for inclusion in the results and neither receives any special handling, but you should know my priors.

What the survey measures

Six questions, all asking for a number you can verify rather than an impression. How many days from grant award to a spendable account. How many days from submitting receipts to being reimbursed. How many days for a purchase order to be approved. How many documents a new vendor has to produce. How many days from a signed offer letter to someone's first day of work.

Impressions of bureaucracy are easy to collect and hard to compare. Dates pulled from your own email are harder to collect and much harder to argue with.

Current status

The ABS opened to responses on [LAUNCH DATE]. Results will be published once enough institutions clear the reporting threshold: at least ten usable responses for a given question before that institution appears in that column, and at least five institutions ready before the first release. Institutions with five to nine responses will be listed separately as provisional.

The table will update periodically as new responses come in. Every published figure will carry a response count and an uncertainty interval, because a median built from ten answers is an estimate and not a verdict.

Right now I'm focusing on gathering responses from universities in the USA.

What comes next

If I get enough interest and this appears to be useful to people, I'll probably make a push for more responses or updated responses every few years, and sunset old data that may no longer be representative. I will also probably update the questions based on feedback, and open it to more countries.

Privacy

Your email address is used once, to check that you work at the institution you named and to stop one person filling in the same university twenty times. After that check, the address is replaced with a one-way cryptographic hash. The hash still lets me spot duplicate submissions; it cannot be turned back into your address.

Nothing you submit is published at the individual level. Results are reported as institution-level medians, and a figure is only published once the institution clears the reporting threshold, so no single response is ever identifiable from the table. Free-text comments are read by me and are not published verbatim without asking you first.

I ask for your broad academic division rather than your department, because institution plus department plus position can identify a specific person in a small unit. I do not sell, share, or license the data, and I will not disclose responses to any institution, including yours.
